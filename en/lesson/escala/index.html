Autoscaling

Black Friday is here. How the cluster grows on its own at peak time and shrinks overnight.

12 minIntermediate11 steps

▶ Open the interactive lesson

This is the text version. The interactive lesson has the animated diagram for each step.

You will learn

  • Tell vertical from horizontal scaling
  • Understand CPU and memory requests
  • Scale pods automatically with the HPA
  • Scale machines with the Cluster Autoscaler
  • Know the limits of autoscaling

Step by step

Step 1 · ContextA quiet Tuesday

A thousand people are browsing the store. Two API pods cope easily, with CPU around 30%: plenty of headroom.

Notice the HPA on the left. It will be the hero of this lesson.

Step 2 · ProblemBlack Friday arrives

Traffic grows fivefold. Worse: lots of people are checking out, and every checkout is a heavy request (the little squares in the diagram).

CPU on both pods goes past 90%, pages get slow and some requests start to fail.

Watch out: A pod at 95% CPU doesn’t fall over all at once. First it gets slower, then come the timeout errors.

Step 3 · Trade-offOption 1: bigger pods

You can give each pod more CPU, from 2 to 4. That’s vertical scaling: making each piece bigger.

But to change its size, the pod has to be recreated. During the swap one pod fewer is serving, and the other sinks even deeper. And there’s a ceiling: a pod can never be bigger than the node it lives on.

Watch out: Vertical scaling has a limit and causes interruptions. It’s for tuning, not for peaks.

Step 4 · SolutionOption 2: more pods

Instead of bigger pods, more pods of the same size. You change the number of replicas from 2 to 4, and the Service shares traffic among them all.

CPU drops to about 50%. That’s horizontal scaling, and it has no hard ceiling: you can keep adding.

Key idea: Scaling out works because the API pods are identical and don’t keep their own data. Any of them can serve any customer.

kubectl scale deployment api --replicas=4

Step 5 · ConceptRequests: booking a table

Each pod declares how much it needs to work, for example half a CPU and 256 MB of memory. That’s called a request.

The scheduler uses that number like a restaurant booking: it only puts a pod on a node that still has room to book. Without requests, it would overfill a node without noticing.

Good to know: There are also limits: the most a pod may use. Go over the memory limit and the pod is killed (that OOMKilled from the pods lesson).

Step 6 · SolutionHPA: the autopilot

Changing the number of replicas by hand works, but nobody stays up watching graphs. The Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA) does it for you.

Every 15 seconds it checks average CPU and compares it with the target, 70% here. Above target, it adds replicas; well below, it removes them.

Key idea: The maths is simple: replicas = current × usage ÷ target. With 4 pods at 95% and a 70% target: 4 × 95 ÷ 70 = 5.4, rounded up to 6 pods.

kubectl autoscale deployment api --cpu-percent=70 --min=2 --max=10

Step 7 · ProblemNo room left

Traffic climbed again and the HPA asked for a seventh pod. But both nodes are full: the bookings (requests) already take up all the space, including those of other apps, in grey.

The new pod is Pending: it exists in the notebook, but the scheduler can’t find a place for it.

Watch out: A Pending pod is a sign that machines are missing, not that the app is broken.

kubectl get pods --field-selector=status.phase=Pending

Step 8 · SolutionThe cluster gets a new machine

The Cluster Autoscaler spots the Pending pod and asks the cloud provider for a new machine (on AWS, Karpenter plays the same role).

A minute or two later, node-3 joins the cluster and the pending pod finally runs on it.

Key idea: The HPA creates pods; the Cluster Autoscaler creates machines. Each needs the other for the peak to fit.

Step 9 · ConceptNight time: time to shrink

At 4 a.m. hardly anyone is shopping. With CPU low, the HPA slowly removes replicas, down to the minimum of 2. It waits about 5 minutes before shrinking, to avoid going up and down.

node-3 is left empty and the Cluster Autoscaler gives it back to the cloud. You only pay for the peak while it lasts.

Step 10 · Trade-offTrade-off: scaling isn’t instant

A flash sale starts at exactly 8 p.m. The HPA reacts in seconds and asks for pods, but they don’t fit; a new node takes 1 to 2 minutes to boot and still has to pull the image.

In the meantime the existing pods struggle, and some customers see errors.

Watch out: If you know a peak is coming (a launch, a TV campaign), raise the minimum replicas beforehand. Autoscaling reacts; it doesn’t predict.

Step 11 · SummarySummary: three ways to scale

The most common pair is HPA plus Cluster Autoscaler: pods grow first, and machines follow.

Good to know: Rule of thumb: set realistic requests, a minimum of 2 replicas and a CPU target between 60% and 70%.
