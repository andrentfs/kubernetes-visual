Isolation and encryption: Secrets, sandboxes and mTLS

Protecting data and separating the teams that share a cluster: Secrets encrypted in etcd, isolated teams, sandboxed containers and encrypted traffic between pods.

Step by step

Step 1 · ContextTwo teams, one cluster

Teams A and B share the cluster, each in its own namespace. Both keep passwords in Secrets, which the API server writes to etcd.

The CKS question is: who can read those passwords, and how much can one team affect the other?

Step 2 · ProblemA Secret is base64, not encryption

By default, the API server writes Secrets to etcd unencrypted. Anyone with access to etcd, or to a backup of it, reads the password directly: etcdctl get /registry/secrets/loja/db-pass.

The base64 in the YAML is just encoding: base64 -d gives back the original text.

Watch out: In the exam, etcdctl needs the certificates: --cacert, --cert and --key. The paths are in the etcd manifest.

etcdctl get /registry/secrets/loja/db-pass

Step 3 · SolutionEncryption at rest

Create an EncryptionConfiguration with the resources (secrets) and the providers in order. The first provider is used for writing: aescbc, aesgcm, secretbox or kms. identity (no encryption) goes last, only to read what already exists.

On the kube-apiserver: --encryption-provider-config pointing to the file, with a volume and volumeMount. New Secrets appear in etcd with the prefix k8s:enc:aescbc:v1:.

Good to know: In production, the documentation recommends the kms provider (v2), with the key kept outside the cluster.

grep encryption-provider-config /etc/kubernetes/manifests/kube-apiserver.yaml

Step 4 · ConceptEncrypting what already existed

The configuration only applies to new writes. Old Secrets stay in plain text until they are written again.

kubectl get secrets -A -o json | kubectl replace -f - rewrites all of them. Then check in etcd that every one has the k8s:enc prefix.

kubectl get secrets -A -o json | kubectl replace -f -

Step 5 · ConceptWho can read Secrets

Encrypting etcd doesn’t protect against anyone with get secrets on the API. Give get and list on secrets only to who needs them, in one namespace. Remember that list also returns the contents.

In the pod, prefer mounting the Secret as a volume over environment variables: variables leak more easily into logs, dumps and child processes.

Key idea: Anyone who can create pods in a namespace can mount any Secret in it. That is Secret access too.

Step 6 · ConceptIsolating the teams

A namespace alone isolates nothing. Combine per-namespace RBAC, a default-deny NetworkPolicy between namespaces, ResourceQuota and LimitRange so one team can’t exhaust the cluster, and Pod Security Standards (enforce=restricted label).

For stronger isolation, give each team its own nodes with taints and nodeSelector, or use separate clusters.

kubectl label ns tenant-a pod-security.kubernetes.io/enforce=restricted

Step 7 · ProblemEveryone shares the same kernel

Containers are processes on the node’s single kernel. A kernel flaw (container escape) can take one pod to the other pods on the same node, including other teams’ pods.

For untrusted code, such as customer builds or third-party plugins, that risk is too high.

Step 8 · SolutionSandboxing with RuntimeClass

Runtimes such as gVisor (runsc) and Kata Containers add a layer between the container and the node’s kernel: a user-space kernel or a lightweight VM.

A RuntimeClass with handler: runsc points to the runtime installed on the node. In the pod, runtimeClassName: gvisor. Other pods keep the normal runtime.

Watch out: The handler must exist in containerd’s configuration on every node, or the pod won’t start.

kubectl get runtimeclass

Step 9 · ConceptEncrypted traffic between pods (mTLS)

By default, traffic between pods isn’t encrypted: anyone capturing packets on the network sees the content.

A service mesh such as Istio adds mTLS between pods (in Istio, PeerAuthentication with mode: STRICT). Cilium can encrypt traffic between nodes with WireGuard or IPsec, without changing the pods.

Step 10 · Trade-offTrade-off: every layer has a cost

A mesh adds a proxy (sidecar or per node), latency and one more component to run. A sandbox makes some calls slower, and not every app is compatible.

Use them where the risk justifies it: sensitive data, real multi-tenancy, third-party code.

Step 11 · SummarySummary: protected data, separate teams

Each risk has its own layer; combine the ones that make sense for your cluster.
