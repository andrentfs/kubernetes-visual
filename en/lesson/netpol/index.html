NetworkPolicy

By default every pod talks to every pod. NetworkPolicies are the firewall between them.

12 minIntermediate10 steps

▶ Open the interactive lesson

This is the text version. The interactive lesson has the animated diagram for each step.

You will learn

  • See the risk of “everything open”
  • Understand how a policy selects pods
  • Apply default deny and open only what’s needed
  • Use namespaceSelector and ports
  • Avoid the AND vs OR mix-up
  • Not break DNS with egress

Step by step

Step 1 · ContextWith no policy, anyone reaches the database

The database is in the dados namespace. The website is in loja. And a forgotten test pod is in teste.

With no NetworkPolicy, all of them can connect to the database, including the test pod. If it’s compromised, the attacker is one step from the data.

Watch out: The Kubernetes network model is “everything talks to everything”. Isolation is your job.

Step 2 · ConceptWhat a NetworkPolicy does

A NetworkPolicy picks pods with podSelector and says what traffic they may receive (ingress) or send (egress).

As soon as a pod is selected by some ingress policy, any incoming traffic not allowed is blocked. Pods not selected stay open.

Watch out: The CNI enforces the rules. With a CNI that lacks support (like plain Flannel), the policy is accepted and simply ignored.

Step 3 · SolutionDefault deny: close everything

The first policy is usually the simplest: podSelector: {} (every pod in the namespace) with policyTypes: [Ingress] and no rules.

Result: nobody gets into any pod in the dados namespace. Not even the website. Now you open only what’s needed.

kubectl apply -f deny-all.yaml

Step 4 · SolutionOpening only for who needs it

A second policy selects the database (podSelector: app=db) and, in ingress.from, allows pods with app=web.

Policies add up: whatever any of them allows, passes. The website talks to the database again; the test pod stays blocked.

Key idea: Policies only allow, never deny. The blocking comes from the pod being selected; the rules open exceptions.

Step 5 · ConceptAllowing by namespace

A podSelector alone in from only looks at the policy’s own namespace. Since the website is in loja, you need a namespaceSelector.

Namespaces get the automatic label kubernetes.io/metadata.name. A namespaceSelector with kubernetes.io/metadata.name: loja allows that namespace’s pods.

Step 6 · ConceptOpening only the right port

In ports, you restrict protocol and port: TCP 5432 for PostgreSQL. The pod’s other ports stay closed, even for whoever is allowed.

That limits the damage if the website is compromised: the attacker can only reach the database on the database port.

Step 7 · Trade-offTrade-off: the AND vs OR trap

Inside one item of from, namespaceSelector and podSelector together mean AND: pods with app=web inside the loja namespace.

In two separate items (with two dashes), they mean OR: any pod in the loja namespace, or any pod with app=web in any namespace. Then the web pod in the teste namespace gets in.

Watch out: One extra dash in the YAML changes the rule completely. Check with kubectl describe networkpolicy.

Step 8 · ProblemEgress: don’t forget DNS

A default-deny egress policy in the loja namespace blocks everything leaving the pods, including queries to CoreDNS.

The website can’t even find the database’s IP. The errors look like naming problems (“could not resolve”), not like a firewall.

Watch out: Every restrictive egress policy must allow UDP and TCP on port 53 to kube-system.

Step 9 · SolutionTesting the policy

Allow DNS (port 53) and the database (5432) in the website’s egress, and test from inside the pod: kubectl exec web -- nc -zv -w 2 db.dados 5432.

A connection blocked by a NetworkPolicy usually doesn’t error right away: it times out. So always use a short timeout when testing.

kubectl exec web -- nc -zv -w 2 db.dados 5432

Step 10 · SummarySummary: building a pod firewall

Close everything with default deny, then open exactly what’s needed, in both directions.
