Health checks: readiness, liveness and startup

How Kubernetes finds out whether an app is ready, alive, or just slow to start.

11 minIntermediate11 steps

▶ Open the interactive lesson

This is the text version. The interactive lesson has the animated diagram for each step.

You will learn

  • Tell running apart from healthy
  • Use readiness to control traffic
  • Use liveness to restart what froze
  • Protect slow apps with a startup probe
  • Avoid the probe that takes everything down

Step by step

Step 1 · ContextRunning doesn’t mean healthy

Two API pods are serving the store. To Kubernetes, a container is “fine” as long as its process is running.

But a process can be running and still unable to serve: still loading, without a database connection, or frozen. On its own, the cluster can’t tell.

Key idea: Kubernetes only knows what you teach it. Probes are the questions you teach the kubelet to ask.

Step 2 · ProblemTraffic arrives too soon

A new pod has just started. It needs about 20 seconds to read its settings and open the database connection.

But as soon as the container is up, the Service starts sending customers to it. Anyone who lands on that pod in those 20 seconds gets an error.

Watch out: Without a readiness probe, “container running” already counts as “ready to serve”.

Step 3 · ConceptProbe: a question asked over and over

A probe is a check the kubelet runs on each container, every so often (every 10 seconds by default).

It comes in four kinds: an HTTP call (like GET /healthz, which must answer with a code between 200 and 399), a TCP connection to a port, a command run inside the container, or a gRPC call.

Good to know: The most used settings: initialDelaySeconds (how long to wait before the first check), periodSeconds (how often), timeoutSeconds (how long to wait for an answer) and failureThreshold (how many failures in a row count as “failed”, 3 by default).

Step 4 · SolutionReadiness: “can you take customers?”

The readiness probe answers a single question: can this pod receive traffic yet?

While the answer is no, the pod stays off the Service’s endpoints. It keeps running; it just gets no customers. Nothing is restarted.

Key idea: Readiness controls traffic, not the pod’s life. Fails: out of the Service. Passes: into the Service.

Step 5 · SolutionPassed the probe, joins the Service

After 20 seconds, the new pod’s /healthz answers 200. The kubelet marks the pod Ready, and the Service starts sending it customers.

Readiness keeps running after that. If the pod loses the database midday, it leaves the list again until it recovers, as you saw in the Service lesson.

Step 6 · ProblemA pod that froze for good

A bug sent one pod into a deadlock: two parts of the program wait for each other forever. The process is still alive, but it never answers anything again.

Readiness takes the pod out of the Service, which protects customers. But it stays there, idle, using memory and a replica slot, and never comes back on its own.

Watch out: Readiness doesn’t restart anything. A frozen pod with only readiness stays out of service forever.

Step 7 · SolutionLiveness: “are you still alive?”

The liveness probe asks something else: is this container still working? If it fails 3 times in a row, the kubelet kills the container and starts it again, in the same pod.

A few seconds later the restarted pod passes readiness and rejoins the Service. The RESTARTS column in kubectl get pods shows how many times this has happened.

Key idea: Liveness restarts the container. Use it for states the app can’t get out of on its own, like deadlocks.

kubectl get pods -w

Step 8 · ProblemThe slow app that never manages to start

A Java reporting service takes 90 seconds to start. Its liveness starts checking at 10 seconds, fails 3 times, and the kubelet restarts the container before it finishes loading.

This repeats forever. The pod ends up in CrashLoopBackOff: Kubernetes waits longer and longer between attempts (10 s, 20 s, 40 s… up to 5 minutes).

Watch out: CrashLoopBackOff is a symptom, not a cause. kubectl describe pod and kubectl logs --previous show what happened before each restart.

kubectl describe pod relatorios

Step 9 · SolutionStartup probe: patience at the start line

The startup probe fixes this. Until it passes, liveness and readiness are switched off.

With failureThreshold: 30 and periodSeconds: 10, the app has up to 300 seconds to start. Once startup passes, it steps aside and the other two take over.

Good to know: That way you keep a fast, strict liveness for everyday use without killing the app while it’s still starting.

Step 10 · Trade-offTrade-off: the probe that takes everything down

Someone made the API’s liveness also check the database. It sounds careful, but the database went down for a minute and every liveness failed at once.

Result: the kubelet restarted every API pod together. When the database came back, the whole API was still starting, and the store was down for longer than the database itself.

Watch out: Liveness should check only the process itself. External dependencies (database, other APIs) belong in readiness, or nowhere.

Step 11 · SummarySummary: three questions, three reactions

Use all three together: startup for slow apps, readiness always, liveness with care.

Good to know: Probes come up a lot in the exam: know httpGet, tcpSocket and exec by heart, and use kubectl explain pod.spec.containers.livenessProbe when you forget a field.
